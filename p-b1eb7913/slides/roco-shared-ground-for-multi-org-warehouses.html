Integrator

The vendor

OEM · WMS · us

The makers

Robot OEMs, software vendors, ROCO itself — each holds a piece of the truth.

Three+ organizations, one shared floor — each holding a piece of the truth, none of them owning the whole. And today they each see a different version of reality.

The problem today

Coordination happens everywhere except the data

  • Today the problem moves over email, calls, Teams threads and spreadsheets — detached from the metric that triggered it.
  • ROCO itself is scoped per organization — so even two orgs on the same site can’t see each other’s issues.
  • By the time a problem reaches the one person who can fix it, the context — the chart, the robot, the moment — is gone.
  • The bill: slow resolution, finger-pointing, and a customer who feels alone on the hard days.
The cost of the status quo

Misalignment is expensive — it just hides on three different bills

Vendor time

Triaging noise

Engineers burn hours on “urgent” tickets that turn out to be expected behaviour — time stolen from the few issues that actually matter.

Lost throughput

Silent faults

A real problem nobody escalated lingers for days. In an automated DC, stopped robots are stopped revenue.

The relationship

“We felt alone every time it broke” is how a flagship account quietly stops being a reference you can use.

The sharpest moment

Warehouse start-up & ramp-up is where it hurts most

  • Commissioning a new site = thousands of unknowns and daily firefights.
  • Integrator and customer are in the room together — but with no shared source of truth.
  • Every anomaly becomes a debate: “is that the robots, the layout, the WMS, or the data?”
  • It’s the highest-stress, highest-churn-risk phase of the whole relationship.
The opening

If ROCO is the shared ground from day one of ramp-up, it becomes infrastructure — not a dashboard you add later.

The insight

The vendor usually knows “that’s expected” faster than the customer knows “that’s a fire.”

Expertise is asymmetric — and that asymmetry is the opportunity, not the problem. The system should make it cheap to apply the vendor’s knowledge to the customer’s observations.

The opportunity

ROCO as the neutral shared ground

A place where every organization on a site flags, classifies, owns and resolves issues against one set of data — instead of arguing across three inboxes. The party that sold you the robots can’t also be the sole scorekeeper of whether they work: ROCO normalizes every vendor — any robot OEM, any WMS — into one schema, so the scoreboard is brand-agnostic and owned by no one.

Flag from anywhere

One tap on the exact KPI, chart, signal or robot — the entire end-to-end flow attached: order → task → robot → event.

Curate, don’t gatekeep

The vendor triages by exception; the system pre-deflects with its own knowledge base.

Own it together

Explicit ownership and hand-off across orgs, with a shared board both sides trust.

The mechanic

Cheap to flag → curated to promote

⚑ Flag (1 tap)→ Auto-deflect (known answer / limitation)→ Vendor swipe-triage→ Promote to ticket→ Own · resolve · verify
  • A flag is a vote/observation — it costs nothing. A ticket is a flag that survived triage.
  • Every flag arrives pinned to the full cross-vendor flow — order → fleet → robot → charging → event. No single vendor sees that end to end; ROCO does — which is why triage takes one second, not two days.
  • Spam dies at the gate, not at the source. Silence dies because flagging is one tap.
  • “Won’t a flag button just create more noise?” No — a flag isn’t work. Auto-deflection and one-second triage mean the vendor only ever sees what survived the gate.

Why this is a growth hook

Multi-org cooperation makes ROCO sticky

Every automated warehouse already has 3+ organizations around it. Win the cooperation layer and you don’t sell seats — you become the room they all meet in.

  • Land at ramp-up: integrators bring their customers into ROCO instead of emailing them PDFs.
  • Expand by org: every new actor on a site is a new seat, not a new sale.
  • Network effects: the resolution knowledge base compounds across sites and customers.
  • No lock-in, by design: the record is vendor-independent — swap the integrator and the shared history of how every problem was solved stays in ROCO.
From dashboard to infrastructure

A reporting tool is replaceable. The shared operating record two companies built together is not.

The go-to-market lever

The customer holds the lever — everyone else benefits

The operator signs for the whole automation project — so the operator, not the integrator, can make “we run on shared ROCO” a condition of the contract. One clause guarantees neutral data interpretation, one shared KPI definition, and cross-vendor benchmarking for the life of the site.

End customer · the buyer

Mandates it

Neutral interpretation they don’t have to take on faith, one KPI definition every vendor is held to, benchmarking across sites & brands, SLA teeth, and no lock-in.

Integrator

Says yes anyway

Auto-deflection kills noise tickets, triage-by-exception saves engineer hours, the neutral record proves they resolved it — and “we work transparently via ROCO” wins bids.

Robot vendor · OEM

Wins by it

Normalized field data across real deployments is product gold — and robots that genuinely perform win on a neutral scoreboard. Neutrality is ammunition, not a threat.

Sell the customer once, at ramp-up — and they pull the rest of the ecosystem in. Distribution solved.
